Training plan

The goal is not motion. The goal is readable team play.

RIG already has the rare part: a deterministic game that can replay a swarm's choices frame-for-frame. The learning plan has to respect what the arena is actually asking for. Four agents need to learn timing, spacing, possession, defense, and when a pass is worth more than a safe hold. That is too much to ask from one cold start self-play run.

The current baseline AI is useful as scaffolding, but it is not an expert. It is a hand-built controller with primitive habits, not a source of polished sport wisdom. Training should borrow its low-level mechanics where they help, then outgrow it.

Start with a curriculum

A good swarm should be taught in stages, because the full game hides the signal. If the first lesson is four-on-four adversarial play, the learner can get rewarded for the easiest stable behavior: keep the bell, avoid risk, and never learn how to advance the cast.

  1. Move and catch Single-rigger drills: grapple, brake, recover, catch a free bell, avoid the skin.
  2. Pass with a receiver Two-rigger drills where the useful action is a lead pass, not a throw at the teammate's current body.
  3. Advance the cast Small-sided possession tasks where reward is signed field progress through the gates, not raw pass count.
  4. Play baseline teams Four learned riggers against the hand-built side, using fixed seeds so every improvement is comparable.
  5. Enter the league Self-play only after the team can already move the bell with purpose.

Raise the action level

Raw aim/reel/fire controls are expressive, but they make the learner discover basic piloting and team tactics at the same time. That is a slow way to get an interesting sport. A better split is tactical intent from the learner, low-level execution from controllers.

Policy chooses intent

Carry, receive, lead-pass to space, mark, screen, recover, pressure, or hold shape.

Controller executes motion

The existing grappling controller can turn intent into line targets, reel choices, catch posture, and throws.

End-to-end remains possible

Once tactics work, lower-level residual actions can let the learner improve on the controller instead of being trapped by it.

Do not make the swarm rediscover hands before it learns the sport.

Use the baseline carefully

The baseline AI is valuable because it knows how to produce legal-ish inputs and keep the match moving. It is not valuable because it plays RIG beautifully. That distinction matters.

  • Good use: behavior cloning for basic mechanics, warm starts, and a stable opponent in early drills.
  • Bad use: treating the baseline as an expert demonstration of scoring strategy.
  • Best use: let the learner imitate enough to stand up, then reward it for beating and surpassing the scaffold.

Train with real gradients

The current native learner proves the loop exists, but evolution strategies over a 24k-parameter attention actor and a 34k-parameter critic are not the serious scaling path. The Rust simulator should do what it is best at: fast, deterministic rollout. PyTorch or JAX should do what they are best at: backpropagation, optimizers, batching, recurrent policies, and diagnostics.

Reward field progress, not hoarding

Keep-away is not a mysterious failure mode. It is the obvious basin when possession and pass chains are easier than scoring. The dense signal should point toward useful possession: advancing the cast, creating a catchable lead, breaking pressure, and turning defense into field position.

Rule of thumb: a pass should be rewarded because it creates an advantage, not because the counter went up.

Make a league, not one champion

A single best policy is not enough for an adversarial sport. It can overfit to the opponent it sees most and forget how to handle older tricks. A league keeps pressure on the swarm from multiple directions: main agents, exploiters, older snapshots, and style-diverse teams.

Main agents

Train against the full distribution and carry the current best general game.

Exploiters

Hunt weaknesses in the main agent so fragile habits get exposed early.

Archive teams

Preserve old and weird strategies so progress does not collapse into one local style.

Judge interesting behavior directly

Winning still matters. But for a watchable swarm, the dashboard should also show what kind of policy is emerging: gate clears, successful lead passes, interceptions, pressure breaks, time-to-shot, possession value, spacing, and diversity of solutions. That is where MAP-Elites-style archives fit: not as the reward, but as a way to keep several high-performing behaviors alive.

The scoreboard says who won. The archive says what kind of team they became.
