Why

More interesting than foosball robots.

Multi-agent coordination is usually studied either in environments too abstract to feel anything about, or on real robots that are slow, expensive, and unforgiving. RIG aims at the gap in between: an arena that is fun to watch, legible enough that you can see why the agents did what they did, lower-stakes than hardware — and still algorithmically faithful to the real problem.

The thesis

Make the hard part — four agents agreeing, under partial information, against an adversary, on sparse reward — into something a person can spectate. Not a loss curve. A match. With a money shot, a culture, a bracket. Keep it deterministic so every claim is reproducible, keep it watchable so progress is visible to anyone, not just to whoever owns the logs.

If you can't watch coordination happen, you can't tell whether it really did.

Where it's heading

  • Stronger swarms — the learned policy and the structured controller keep climbing, on the same seeds, where the gains are honestly comparable.
  • Deeper canon — more of the league, more of the culture a sport accretes, because legibility includes being something you want to follow.
  • A sharper lab — better Gym ergonomics, cleaner judges, more of the experiment exposed to anyone curious enough to look.
  • Built in the open-experiment way — humans and AI agents, isolated work, green gates, validated by watching.

It's a living thing

None of this is finished, and that's the offer, not the apology. RIG is an experiment you can check on. The next version will be better and you'll be able to see exactly how, frame for frame, on the same seed as the last one. That's a rare thing to be able to say about a system that's still being built.

A sport that doesn't exist, teaching us about a problem that does — and you get to watch it think.
