Canon

The game played in the calm.

RIG is played inside a spinning O'Neill cylinder — a habitat 640 metres long and 45 metres to the skin, turning slowly enough that the air near the central axis carries no weight at all. That weightless core is the calm. The match happens there.

You cannot fly. You can only pull.

This is the rule the whole sport hangs from. A rigger has no thrust, no jets, no wings. The only way to move is the grapple: a claw fired down a line, anchored to structure or to another body, and reeled in by a powered winch. Movement is a problem of where you choose to attach and how hard you pull — closer to an arena shooter's grapple than to swimming.

Let go at the wrong instant and you are a thrown stone for the rest of the play. Let go at the right one and you have just bought a trajectory nobody can take from you.

No engines. No wings. Just a line, a winch, and the nerve to release at the right moment.

The ball is a bell.

There is no ball. There is a bell: a tumbling six-degree-of-freedom object with its own angular momentum. You do not track it cleanly with your eyes as it spins through the dark — you read it by ear. Caught and spun clean, it chimes true, a held tone. Clipped, jostled, struck off-axis, it clatters — and a clattering bell is a bell that is about to be lost.

Possession is not "who holds it." It's who can keep it ringing.

How you score

Points are not "in the goal." They are recognised kinds of contact — the path the bell took when it scored matters as much as that it scored.

  • Fall — driven outward, with the spin's apparent gravity. The brute, honest score.
  • Rise — sent inward toward the axis, against the pull. Harder, prettier, worth more in the watching.
  • Loop — a closed, curved orbit carried through a ring, bent by the spinning frame itself. The money shot. The thing the highlight reels are made of.
  • Curl — a tight controlled bend off a grapple-assisted swing; touch and craft over power.
  • Ground — settled against structure on your terms. The patient, defensive points.
A goal is a fact. A Loop is a story.

The league and The Jump

RIG canon carries a 32-team league — distinct identities, distinct cultures, the way a sport accumulates them over seasons it never actually had. The season converges on a single-elimination bracket: The Jump. One ladder, no rematches, the calm getting quieter as the field thins.

It is fiction, written like it isn't. That's the point — a sport real enough to care about, simple enough to study.
